### Concept
- *What is it:* a prospective experimental study in human participants (including healthy volunteers and eligible patients) designed to evaluate the effects of one or more treatments on health outcomes
- *What does it constitute:* 
	- **Eligibility criteria**:
		- *What is it:* predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria used to determine whether a human participant is eligible for participation in the trial
		- *Why we need precise eligibility criteria:*
			- Improve external validity *i.e.* enhance the extent to which the trial results can be generalised to the target population
			- Ensure patient safety. *Reason:* some patients may be at higher risk of adverse effects from the treatment being studied *e.g.* pregnant women if the drug may harm the foetus
			- Improve internal validity *i.e.* ensure the observed treatment effect can be attributed to the treatment rather than to other factors such as **bias** and **confounding**
		- *How should we define precise eligibility criteria:* consider
			- Source of patient recruitment *e.g.* only in-patients recruited from hospitals for a study of depression may form an atypical patient population
			- Disease state under investigation *e.g.* some disease conditions may vary considerably in severity or diagnostic definition, which can complicate patient selection
			- Specific criteria for exclusion of patients *e.g.* restrict entry to previously untreated patients to avoid diminishing / attenuation of the treatment effect
	- **Treatment**, **Standard treatment**, **placebo**: 
		- *What is treatment:* a condition or intervention assigned to participants for comparison. Examples include drug therapies (e.g., different drugs or dosages), surgical procedures, cancer therapies, behavioural interventions, and patient-management strategies.
		- *What is standard treatment:* the treatment currently being used and considered effective on the basis of existing evidence
		- *What is placebo:* an inactive substance or sham treatment designed to resemble the active intervention but lacking its specific therapeutic effect
	- **Treatment group** & **Control group**
		- *What is treatment group:* the group of subjects receiving the same new treatment or intervention
		- *What is control group:* the group of participants used for comparison. Depending on the study design, it may receive standard treatment, placebo, no treatment, or experimental drug
- *What we do:* compare outcomes between the treatment group and the control group
### Example
- **Randomised controlled trial (RCT)**, also called a randomised concurrently controlled clinical trial. It is a clinical trial in which eligible participants are randomly allocated to different treatment groups, such as an experimental treatment group and a control group, to evaluate the effect of an intervention while minimising bias and confounding
### Dimension
#### Randomised
- *What is it:* participants satisfying the eligibility criteria are allocated to treatment groups at random
#### Controlled
- *What is it:* one or more treatment groups are compared with a control group
#### Blinding
##### Single-blind
- *What is it:* a trial in which patients do not know which treatment they are receiving
##### Double-blind
- *What is it:* a trial in which neither the patients, physicians, nor evaluators are aware of which treatment the patients are receiving
#### Phases
- Can we give it safely? → Phase I
- Does it appear to work? → Phase II
- Does it truly work better than current care? → Phase III
- What happens in real-world use over years? → Phase IV
##### Phase I
- *Size:* a small number of healthy volunteers (usually) or patients (sometimes) *e.g*. 20 to 80. However, if a new drug is intended for use in cancer patients, researchers conduct Phase 1 studies in patients with that type of cancer
- *Length of study:* several months
- *Success rate:* approximately 70% of drugs move to the next phase
- *Purpose:* learn about safety (including side effects), dose finding, **pharmacokinetics** (in short PK *i.e.* how the body handles the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, elimination)
##### Phase II
- *Size:* up to several hundred patients *e.g.* 100 to 300
- *Length of study:* several months to 2 years
- *Success rate:* approximately 33% of drugs move to the next phase
- *Purpose:* determine its effectiveness and to further study its safety
##### Phase III
- Aka **pivotal studies**
- *Size:* several hundred to several thousand patients *e.g.* 300 to 3,000. often conducted across multiple centres or hospitals
- *Length of study:* 1 to 4 years
- *Success rate:* approximately 25-30% of drugs move to the next phase
- *Purpose:* confirm its effectiveness, monitor side effects, compare it with standard or similar treatments, and collect information that will allow the new drug or treatment to be used safely
##### Phase IV
- *Size:* several thousand patients
- *When it takes place:* after the drug is approved and made available to the public
- *Purpose:* track the safety (*i.e.* **pharmacovigilance**) in the general population, seek more information about a drug or treatment's benefits, and optimal use
