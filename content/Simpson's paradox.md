### Concept
- *What is it:* a reversal of the direction of a comparison or an association when data from several groups are combined to form a single group.
- *Why it is important:* It highlights how aggregating data can sometimes lead to misleading conclusions
- *When it happens:* usually appears in **observational studies** in which the experimental units had chosen the treatment on their own instead of being assigned by the experimenter
### Example
#### Example 1
- Consider data from a study examining the relationship between smoking and 20-year survival rates of women (Appleton et al., 1996)
- The data suggest that smoking might be beneficial because 43% of non-smokers died compared to 38% of smoker *i.e.* smoking would result in a decreasing risk of death 
![[Simpson's paradox - example - without confounding factor.png|402]]
- *Problem:* The above result does not show a correct interpretation of the data because it ignored an important **confounding variable** that is strongly related to smoking and survival - women's ages at the start of the study
- When survival rates are determined for women in each of the 10-year age ranges (15-54, 55-64, 65-74), the non-smoking group does better in each case
